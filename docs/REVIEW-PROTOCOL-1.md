# Review of `kiwi/1`, Draft 1 (REVIEW-PROTOCOL-1)

Status: independent security review, 2026-11-19. The reviewer did not write the protocol.
Reviewed: `docs/PROTOCOL.md` (Draft 1, at commit `0e35ec6`), `docs/THREAT-MODEL.md` (Draft 1), `docs/SPIKE-REPORT.md`.
References: "P x.y" is a section of `PROTOCOL.md`, "TM" is the threat model. Neither document was changed.

Severity scale used here:

- **Critical**: a sole copy can be lost, or authentication is broken, with no further fault. (None found.)
- **High**: a stated security property is broken, or a design gap that cannot be repaired after 1.0 ships
  because installed clients would have to understand the repair.
- **Medium**: exploitable, or a data-safety gap that needs one more condition; or an ambiguity that leads two
  implementations to different behaviour or to a state with no exit.
- **Low**: hardening, consistency, wording.

## Summary

The cryptographic core is sound: the QR binding, the SAS commit-then-reveal ordering, the exporter-bound
signed transcript and the keyed second-verification proof all do what the text claims (details under
"What holds"). The weak points are elsewhere: unbounded counters received from the peer (one of them
defeats the weekly check outright), ledger rollback that no rule can see, a release-key scheme with no
revocation, library defaults that contradict the "no resumption" claim, several Android 11+ trash edge
cases, and PC-side cleanup rules that can reach a committed file. Counts: Critical 0, High 3, Medium 17,
Low 14.

## What holds (checked, no finding)

- **QR pairing (P 2.3-2.4).** PH pins `pc_id` before it sends its certificate; PC authenticates PH by a MAC
  under a 128-bit one-time secret bound to `E_pair`, both ids and both pairing messages. A relay cannot
  reuse a proof (the exporter differs per connection), and role bytes `0x01`/`0x02` stop reflection.
- **SAS commitment (P 2.5).** A man in the middle must fix `nonce_p'` (commit) towards PC before PC's
  `nonce_c` exists, and must send `nonce_c'` to PH before PH reveals `nonce_p`. Whatever order M uses, the
  SAS of the connection M finishes last is uniform from M's point of view at M's last free choice.
  Grinding the TLS exporter (M chooses its own key shares) does not help because both nonces are drawn
  after the handshake. Success 10^-6 per attempt; modulo bias of `u64 mod 10^6` is about 10^-13. M cannot
  make both screens agree; the weak point is the human step (KT-06).
- **Domain separation.** All `KDF` contexts are distinct; every hash input is fixed-length or
  length-prefixed; the session signature prefix `kiwi/1 session auth` cannot collide with a TLS 1.3
  `CertificateVerify` input (64 bytes `0x20`) or an X.509 TBS (`0x30`); the two exporter labels differ and
  are never used on the same connection. No cross-protocol use of the identity key was found.
- **Downgrade.** One ALPN, TLS 1.3 only, QUIC v1 only, no fallback; `minor` and `caps` are inside the
  signed transcript. No downgrade path found.
- **Keyed proof (P 8.5).** `K_v` binds nonce, both ids, stage, hash and size; BLAKE3 keyed mode needs every
  content byte, so a stale ledger, a cached hash or a replayed answer cannot pass. The remaining exposure
  is after the proof (KT-09), not in it.
- **Weekly check, design level (P 3.3).** `age = max(both sides)` means one honest device defeats a clock
  rollback on the other; restored state on either side goes to `REQUIRED`; expiry itself deletes nothing.
  The arithmetic is the problem (KT-01, KT-08).
- **Commit order (P 7).** Flush, unbuffered re-read, intent row, create-new rename, ledger commit, then
  receipt: correct, except rename durability (KT-13).

A holder of a copied phone key (T3, exactly the adversary 3.1 says the check exists for) sends `confirm_age = 2^64-1`: the PC stores `confirm_lc = LC - age = LC + 1 (mod 2^64)`, and from the next evaluation its own `confirm_age = LC' - confirm_lc` is near 0, so the link is `CONFIRMED` for another 7 days with nobody at the PC screen. **Q1 in detail (Public versus Private).** Private-only is not the safer policy in practice. Windows 11
classifies every new network as Public, USB tethering often appears as a new network for each tethering
session, and changing the category may need administrator rights. Users who are told "set this network to
Private" do it for networks they do not control, which also opens network discovery and file sharing on
that network: a larger exposure than the one Private-only avoids. The authentication of `kiwi/1` does not
depend on the network category. What Public adds is reach from more hostile links to the pre-authentication
surface (Retry, QUIC/TLS handshake, our verifier) and the ability of any prober there to fetch the PC's
certificate (R7, tracking across public places). The opt-in per network, "pinned peers only" (no pairing
window of method 2, no `pm=1`), on-link source filtering, hop limit 1 and Retry keep that addition small.
Domain networks stay excluded. The installer must create the rule as a machine-wide install; a per-user
install leaves the decision to Windows' own prompt, where "Cancel" creates a block rule that overrides
any allow rule, so the program must detect a block and explain it.
